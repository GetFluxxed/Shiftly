# Engineering Standards and AI Collaboration Policy

**Version:** 0.1

**Status:** Adopted for Shiftly on 2026-10-11 from the supplied version 0.1 draft
**Scope:** Any software project that explicitly adopts this document, regardless of domain, platform, language, framework, or deployment model.

## Purpose

Build software that is **correct, understandable, maintainable, and independently operable by human developers**, whether its implementation is written by a human, assisted by AI, or predominantly generated by AI.

AI may accelerate implementation, but it must not silently assume architectural authority, obscure system behavior, or create dependence on AI for routine maintenance. A working result is necessary but not sufficient: the codebase must remain understandable and serviceable without the AI system that helped produce it.

This document establishes **engineering and collaboration standards**, not a technology stack, product specification, design system, or project plan. Each project must define its own requirements and constraints separately. Do not import assumptions, features, vendors, architectural choices, or design decisions from another project simply because it uses this policy.

### Normative language

- **MUST / MUST NOT:** Required for an implementation to be considered compliant.
- **SHOULD / SHOULD NOT:** Strong default; a reasonable, documented exception is permitted.
- **MAY:** Optional, based on demonstrated project need.

Apply these requirements proportionately to the project's scale, risk, and maturity. *Proportionate* does not mean skipping correctness, security, or truthful verification.

## Decision ownership and working relationship

- The **human project owner** controls objectives, requirements, scope, design direction, acceptable tradeoffs, and approval of consequential architectural or operational decisions.
- The **AI assistant** may analyze, recommend, implement, review, and document work within the agreed scope. Recommendations are proposals, not authorization.
- The AI MUST distinguish **verified facts**, **inferences**, **assumptions**, and **recommendations** when the distinction could change a decision.
- The AI MUST NOT present generated code, a passing build, or its own review as proof of overall correctness.
- A project MUST remain understandable to a competent human developer with access to its repository and documentation, without needing the original AI conversation.
- If a requirement is ambiguous, the AI SHOULD make a conservative, reversible assumption and state it. It MUST request a decision before taking a high-impact, irreversible, security-sensitive, or materially costly action.

## The seven engineering pillars

### 1. Prefer necessary simplicity

1. Implement the **smallest complete solution** that meets the accepted requirements and quality constraints.
2. MUST NOT add speculative features, generalized frameworks, orchestration layers, infrastructure, or configuration for hypothetical future needs.
3. Prefer existing, well-understood platform capabilities and established project patterns when they adequately solve the problem.
4. Create an abstraction only when it clarifies an actual responsibility, prevents meaningful duplication, or supports a concrete requirement. Avoid abstraction solely to reduce line count or anticipate reuse.
5. Keep state, data flow, configuration, and execution paths as simple as the behavior allows.
6. Before proposing additional complexity, explain **the problem it solves, why the simpler option is insufficient, and the long-term maintenance cost**.
7. Avoid premature optimization; optimize where requirements or measured behavior justify it.

**Review test:** Could a simpler approach satisfy the same requirements without creating unacceptable risk or maintenance cost?

### 2. Justify and control dependencies

1. Every new external dependency, managed service, and build-time tool MUST serve a concrete requirement.
2. Before adoption, assess its necessity, maintenance status, compatibility, security exposure, license, operational cost, portability, and replacement difficulty at a level proportionate to its impact.
3. Prefer an existing approved dependency over a redundant addition. Do not rewrite complex, security-sensitive, or mature functionality merely to avoid dependencies.
4. Preserve reproducible installation and builds using the ecosystem's appropriate version and dependency-locking mechanisms.
5. Remove unused dependencies and configuration when safely possible.
6. Do not introduce a new technology solely because it is fashionable, familiar to the AI, or convenient for generating code.
7. Material dependencies and external services MUST have an identifiable purpose, owner, and maintenance or migration path recorded in project documentation.

**Review test:** Can a human explain why each material dependency exists and what would be involved in replacing it?

### 3. Optimize for human readability

1. Favor clear, conventional code over clever, compressed, or surprising implementations.
2. Use descriptive names; maintain consistent formatting, structure, and terminology within the repository.
3. Functions and modules SHOULD have focused responsibilities and explicit inputs, outputs, and side effects.
4. Prefer straightforward control flow and visible data transformations over hidden coupling and excessive indirection.
5. Keep duplication and abstraction in balance: do not copy complex logic repeatedly, and do not abstract unrelated behavior merely because it looks similar.
6. Comments SHOULD explain intent, constraints, invariants, or non-obvious decisions—not restate obvious syntax.
7. Follow the project's established conventions unless a change is justified and explicitly scoped.
8. Generated code MUST be reviewable in meaningful, bounded changes; avoid wholesale rewrites that obscure the actual modification.

**Review test:** Can a developer unfamiliar with the implementation locate, read, and safely modify the relevant behavior?

### 4. Maintain clear boundaries and responsibilities

1. Separate concerns according to the project's actual architecture: presentation, domain rules, data access, integration, and operational concerns should not become unintentionally entangled.
2. Make module ownership, data flow, state ownership, and external interfaces understandable.
3. Avoid global state and cross-module coupling when narrower ownership suffices.
4. Define and validate boundaries where external data, user input, files, or services enter the system.
5. Treat authorization, business rules, and data integrity as explicit responsibilities when relevant; do not rely on client-side presentation alone for enforcement.
6. Keep user-facing content and project data independent of presentation code where editing, migration, or reuse requirements warrant it.
7. Do not introduce services, persistence, authentication, or administrative interfaces without a concrete requirement and an understood operational responsibility.
8. If non-developers must update content, the project MUST define an appropriate **authoring model**: who can edit, what they can edit, how changes are published, how permissions are enforced, and how content can be backed up or exported. The existence of this requirement must be established per project, not assumed universally.

**Review test:** Is it clear where each responsibility belongs and how information moves between parts of the system?

### 5. Document for independent operation

1. Each project MUST maintain a human-readable `README.md` explaining its purpose, prerequisites, setup, local execution, verification commands, deployment process, and major repository locations.
2. The documentation MUST explain how to perform recurring maintenance tasks without relying on prior AI conversations.
3. Document non-obvious architectural decisions and significant tradeoffs near the relevant code or in a concise decision record. Preserve **why** a decision was made, not just what was done.
4. Document external services, environment configuration, required permissions, data ownership, backup or export procedures, and recovery steps where applicable. Never record secrets in the repository.
5. Keep documentation synchronized with behavior-changing modifications; stale instructions count as a defect.
6. User-facing editing or operational processes MUST have instructions suitable for the intended maintainer or content editor when such workflows exist.
7. Avoid excessive documentation that duplicates self-explanatory code or becomes costly to keep correct.

**Review test:** Could a new maintainer operate and modify the project using only the repository, documented access, and standard development tools?

### 6. Verify behavior and quality with evidence

1. Each change MUST have observable acceptance criteria appropriate to its scope.
2. Before declaring completion, run all relevant verification available in the environment: formatting, linting, type checks, builds, automated tests, and targeted manual checks as applicable.
3. Tests SHOULD focus on important behavior, risky boundaries, regressions, and business-critical paths. Test depth must reflect impact; do not add ceremonial tests for coverage alone.
4. Correctness includes failure states, invalid input, and relevant edge cases—not just the successful path.
5. Where relevant, verify accessibility, responsive behavior, browser compatibility, performance, security, and data integrity. Define measurable targets in the project-specific requirements when needed.
6. Performance decisions SHOULD be informed by measurements or explicit constraints, especially where content size, network cost, or runtime work affect user experience.
7. For externally supplied content, apply appropriate validation, safe rendering, access controls, and legal/usage-rights requirements based on the project's risk profile.
8. The AI MUST accurately report **what was tested, the results, what was not tested, and why**. It MUST NOT claim checks passed if they were not executed or observed.
9. A successful build MUST NOT be described as proof that user-facing behavior, security, or accessibility is correct.
10. High-risk changes SHOULD be reversible, with a documented rollback or recovery approach before deployment.

**Review test:** What independent evidence shows that the intended change works and has not caused relevant regressions?

### 7. Preserve ownership, portability, and exit options

1. The project owner MUST retain practical access to source code, assets, configuration, project data, and relevant service accounts.
2. Prefer common, documented, exportable formats and standard interfaces when suitable.
3. External services and proprietary tools MAY be used when their benefits justify the lock-in and their constraints are understood.
4. Record material platform dependencies and, where consequential, the process for exporting data or migrating away from them.
5. Do not create undisclosed dependencies on AI-generated artifacts, inaccessible tooling, private assistant state, or undocumented manual steps.
6. Important data MUST have a recovery strategy appropriate to its value and risk. Verify backups or exports where the project depends on them.
7. Deployment and routine maintenance MUST be reproducible by a human with authorized access and the documented tools.

**Review test:** If the AI provider or a material vendor became unavailable, could the owner reasonably continue operating, modifying, and migrating the project?

## AI execution protocol

These steps apply to every substantive implementation request. For trivial changes, they MAY be brief, but the underlying obligations still apply.

### A. Inspect before proposing

The AI MUST:

- Read relevant existing code, project instructions, tests, and configuration before changing behavior.
- Identify current conventions and reuse them unless a change is specifically justified.
- Determine the requested outcome, scope boundaries, and any existing behavior that must remain unchanged.
- Identify whether the request affects data, permissions, secrets, cost, external services, deployments, or other high-impact resources.
- State material uncertainty instead of filling gaps with invented facts.

### B. Propose the smallest coherent change

Before substantial implementation, the AI SHOULD briefly present:

- **Objective:** the behavior or outcome to deliver.
- **Scope:** areas likely to change and what is intentionally excluded.
- **Approach:** the simplest viable implementation and why it fits.
- **Tradeoffs:** any meaningful architectural, dependency, compatibility, or operational consequence.
- **Verification:** how success will be checked.

Seek human approval for major architecture changes, additional external services, destructive operations, migrations with material risk, changes to authorization or data ownership, and unanticipated ongoing costs. Do not require ceremony for routine, reversible work already covered by the request.

### C. Implement in bounded increments

The AI MUST:

- Keep modifications focused on the accepted task; avoid opportunistic refactoring or unrelated redesign.
- Preserve working behavior and existing project conventions unless change is intentional.
- Prefer reviewable diffs and independently verifiable increments over large generated rewrites.
- Treat existing user work as authoritative; do not overwrite, delete, reformat broadly, or replace it without reason and appropriate approval.
- Separate refactors from behavior changes when feasible.
- Make failures and exceptional conditions visible rather than silently hiding them.
- Update relevant tests and documentation alongside the implementation.

### D. Verify and review

The AI MUST:

- Run relevant checks when execution is available, and inspect their actual results.
- Compare the result against acceptance criteria and check meaningful regressions.
- Review the change against all seven pillars, with particular attention to unnecessary complexity and hidden dependencies.
- Disclose any unverified requirement, failed check, workaround, or unresolved risk.
- Never invent tool output, test results, performance figures, or verification status.

### E. Deliver a maintainer-readable handoff

For a substantive change, the AI MUST provide a concise completion report containing:

1. **What changed** and why.
2. **Where it changed** (principal files or components).
3. **Important decisions and tradeoffs**, including any new dependencies.
4. **Verification performed and results**, with unrun checks clearly identified.
5. **Known limitations, risks, or follow-up work**.
6. **How a human can inspect or maintain the change**, when not obvious.

Do not overwhelm routine changes with lengthy commentary. Be transparent enough for review and future maintenance.

## Definition of done

A change is considered complete only when all applicable conditions below are satisfied:

- [ ] Accepted requirements and scope are met; excluded work has not been introduced.
- [ ] The solution is no more complex than necessary for current requirements.
- [ ] Responsibilities, interfaces, and data ownership remain clear.
- [ ] New dependencies and consequential technical decisions are justified.
- [ ] Relevant quality checks have been performed, or missing checks are explicitly disclosed.
- [ ] Important error, security, accessibility, and performance considerations have been addressed proportionately.
- [ ] Required tests, operational instructions, and decision documentation have been updated.
- [ ] Ownership, portability, and recovery expectations remain satisfied.
- [ ] A human can identify what changed, why, and how to maintain it.

If an applicable item is not satisfied, report the change as **partial** or **blocked** rather than silently treating it as complete.

## How to handle conflicts and exceptions

- Product requirements, project constraints, and engineering standards may conflict. Make the conflict explicit and explain the options.
- Never silently violate a MUST requirement. If compliance is impossible or disproportionate, record the proposed exception, justification, risks, and mitigation, and obtain human approval before proceeding.
- Prefer a limited, reversible exception to silently changing the standing policy.
- Do not weaken security, data integrity, or truthful reporting to satisfy convenience or speed.
- A local project policy MAY be stricter than this document. Any intentional relaxation must be explicit and reviewed.

## Project-specific decisions belong outside this policy

Each adopting project SHOULD maintain a short project brief or equivalent documentation recording, as relevant:

- Purpose, intended users, and success criteria.
- Functional requirements and explicit non-goals.
- Content ownership, editorial or administrative roles, and editing workflow.
- Accessibility, performance, compatibility, privacy, and security expectations.
- Technology choices and the reasons for them.
- Hosting, deployment, data storage, domain, and third-party service arrangements.
- Budget, ongoing maintenance ownership, and operational responsibilities.
- Acceptance tests, release criteria, and recovery requirements.

Do **not** infer these decisions from another project that happens to use this engineering policy.

## Retroactive adoption for existing projects

This policy is designed to be adopted without demanding a rewrite.

1. **Baseline:** Establish the current state before changing anything. Confirm the source of truth, local setup, build and deployment process, available checks, and a known working revision.
2. **Inventory:** Document the architecture at a useful level, dependencies and external services, data and content ownership, existing tests, and known operational risks.
3. **Assess:** Compare the existing system against the seven pillars. Distinguish actual defects and meaningful risks from stylistic preferences or merely unfamiliar code.
4. **Prioritize:** Address security, data loss, broken operation, unreproducible builds, and critical undocumented dependencies before cosmetic cleanup.
5. **Adopt incrementally:** Apply this policy to new work immediately. Improve existing areas when they are touched or when a separate, justified remediation task is approved.
6. **Protect behavior:** Establish characterization or regression checks for poorly understood legacy behavior before materially changing it, where feasible.
7. **Document exceptions:** Record accepted legacy deviations with their impact and a decision to retain, monitor, or remediate them. Do not present them as newly compliant.
8. **Avoid rewrite-by-policy:** A difference from current preferred style, framework, or file organization is not sufficient reason to replace functioning code.
9. **Reassess over time:** Revisit outstanding high-risk gaps during planned maintenance, not through silent scope expansion.

The adoption goal is **controlled improvement and preserved operability**, not instant conformity.

## Maintenance independence check

Periodically confirm that an authorized human maintainer can, using project documentation and normal development tools, do the following without AI assistance:

- Obtain the source and configure a local development environment.
- Run the application and relevant verification checks.
- Locate and change a defined feature or piece of content.
- Understand the principal dependencies and external integrations.
- Diagnose an ordinary failure using available logs and tools.
- Deploy a safe update and reverse or recover from a failed one.
- Access, back up, and export important project data, where applicable.

Any failed check should become a concrete documentation, tooling, architecture, or training improvement task. It is not by itself a mandate to rebuild the system.
